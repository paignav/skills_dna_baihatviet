# Source Rules

Song-origin research is unusually prone to attractive false stories. Treat the research as content development with fact-checking.

## Required Source Behavior

- Search beyond the first summary result when origin, inspiration, or "true story" claims matter.
- Track who is making each claim: creator, performer, label, journalist, fan, database, or anonymous repost.
- If sources conflict, state the conflict instead of merging the stories.
- If a claim says "inspired by a true story", identify whose statement supports that claim.
- If a song is connected to a film, distinguish whether it was written for the film, reused by the film, popularized by the film, or only associated later.
- If the origin is uncertain, write "không tìm được nguồn đáng tin xác nhận" or equivalent rather than filling the gap.

## Red Flags

Be skeptical of:

- "everyone knows" claims without attribution;
- viral posts retelling a tragedy or romance;
- unsourced translation notes;
- fan wiki pages with no citations;
- YouTube comments used as evidence;
- copied biographies on lyrics sites;
- claims that a song was written for one person, one death, or one event without a creator interview or strong publication.

## Useful Queries

When web research is available, combine the song title with:

- interview composer songwriter inspiration
- liner notes
- making of
- soundtrack interview
- director composer interview
- original release
- official
- obituary profile
- oral history

For non-English songs, search in the original language and English when possible. Preserve the original title in the final answer.

## Confidence Labels

Use concise labels:

- **Cao:** primary source or multiple strong independent sources.
- **Vừa:** reputable secondary source, but no direct creator statement.
- **Thấp:** plausible but weakly sourced, conflicting, or mostly repeated online.
